Private Credit · Story I · New concept

Lending Against Revenue That AI Might Take Away

The private credit industry has published a robust defence of itself. Its averages look fine. Its distribution, and its software book, deserve a closer look.

From our credit desk

The News

T22 Oct. — The Alternative Credit Council has published a report arguing that market fears about private credit are “divorced from reality”. It says industry assets grew 17% year on year to $4.1tn, but it also concedes that roughly one in six surveyed funds reports non-accruals above 5%. Separately, Bloomberg reports that direct lending deal volume had its worst quarter in more than three years. Lenders to software companies seen as exposed to AI disruption are asking for extra covenants and wider spreads, and some are refusing to extend maturities.

The Plumbing

1. The recurring-revenue loan

Much of the software lending done in 2019–2022 used recurring-revenue loans (ARR loans). These are loans to companies with little or no EBITDA, sized as a multiple of annual recurring revenue, typically 1.5–3× ARR. They usually carry a liquidity covenant and a maximum debt-to-ARR covenant. After two to three years they “flip” to a normal EBITDA-based leverage covenant, on the assumption that the company will have grown into profitability by then.

The underwriting rests on retention. Lenders accept a loan with no earnings cover because subscription revenue is expected to stay sticky. Gross revenue retention of around 90% or more is the usual comfort level. AI disruption threatens that assumption directly. If AI agents reduce the number of user seats a customer needs, or a cheaper AI-native product replaces the software, ARR falls through churn, not through a recession. The loan is then sized against a base that is shrinking.

Recovery is affected twice. When an ARR lender enforces, what it recovers is the enterprise value, priced as a multiple of ARR. AI risk shrinks ARR and lowers the multiple buyers will pay for it, so the loss given default rises from both sides.

2. Why the average hides the tail

An industry-wide non-accrual rate can look calm while a sixth of funds are above 5%, because losses are concentrated in particular sectors and vintages. Two technical points matter here:

  • The denominator. Non-accruals at fair value shrink as the loans are marked down, so a fund that has written its bad loans down hard reports a lower non-accrual rate. Non-accruals at cost do not shrink this way.
  • The definition. Loans that have been amended, extended or switched to PIK stay on accrual. A fund can therefore report low non-accruals while carrying loans that are already being worked out.

NAcost = cost of non-accrual loans ÷ total cost    vs    NAFV = fair value of non-accruals ÷ total fair value

Why It Matters for a Fund Analyst

  • Ask for ARR-loan exposure separately: its share of the book, the dates the covenants flip to EBITDA, and how many have flipped on schedule.
  • Ask for borrowers' retention data (gross and net revenue retention), not just revenue growth.
  • Ask for non-accruals at cost, plus loans amended or switched to PIK since origination, by vintage.
  • Check the marks: compare marks on the same software borrowers across BDC schedules of investments.

The Drill · Story I

1. A software borrower has $100m of debt against $50m of ARR. Its maximum debt-to-ARR covenant is 2.33×. ARR then falls 15% through churn. What happens to the covenant?

  1. No breach; leverage is fixed at 2.0× because it is set at close.
  2. Breach; debt-to-ARR is about 2.35×.
  3. No breach; debt-to-ARR is 2.30×.
  4. Not tested until the covenant flips to EBITDA.

2. A fund’s portfolio cost is $1,000m and its fair value is $960m. Non-accrual loans cost $50m and are marked at $25m. The manager quotes non-accruals as “2.6%”. Which figure should you use to compare this fund with its peers?

  1. 2.6%, because fair value is the GAAP measure.
  2. 2.5%, the fair value of non-accruals over total cost.
  3. 5.0%, non-accruals at cost, alongside the fair-value figure.
  4. 5.2%, the cost of non-accruals over total fair value.

3. Industry non-accruals look modest, but one in six funds reports more than 5%. In due diligence, which question best separates the two kinds of fund?

  1. “What is your portfolio’s weighted-average spread?”
  2. “What is your annualised default rate since inception?”
  3. “Which loans, by vintage, have been amended, extended or switched to PIK since origination, and at what marks?”
  4. “How much has AUM grown year on year?”
Answers — tap here, or turn the page · Story I

1. B — breach at about 2.35×.

100 ÷ (50 × 0.85) = 100 ÷ 42.5 = 2.353×, which is above 2.33×.

C multiplies 2.0 by 1.15. A 15% fall in the denominator raises the ratio by 1 ÷ 0.85 = 17.6%, not by 15%. A treats leverage as fixed at close, but it is tested on current ARR. D: ARR covenants are tested until the flip; that is the whole point of them.

2. C — 5.0% at cost, alongside the fair-value figure.

At cost: 50 ÷ 1,000 = 5.0%. At fair value: 25 ÷ 960 = 2.6%. The fair-value figure falls as the manager marks the bad loans down, so harsher marks produce a better headline. Measured at cost, funds are compared on how many loans have stopped paying, whatever their marks.

A is valid for the accounts but distorted for comparing funds. B and D mix the two bases.

3. C — amendments, extensions and PIK switches, by vintage.

Stress in private credit shows up first as amendments, not defaults. Asking for them by vintage and with their marks shows the problems a low non-accrual figure can hide.

A: a high spread can be a symptom of risk as easily as compensation for it. B: defaults since inception are diluted by benign years and miss amended loans. D: fast growth tells you about fundraising, not credit quality, and can even dilute the visible stress.

Rates · Central Banks · Story II

Six Days to the Fed, and a Curve Coiled Tight

The front end has barely moved all month, while the long end sits near its highest level since 2002. Both are telling you something different.

From our rates desk

The News

W22 Oct. — With the Fed meeting on 28 October, futures priced roughly an 82% chance of no change in rates as of 20 October. BMO expects a hold now and a hike in December. It notes that the 2-year yield has closed between 4.70% and 4.82% in every session this month, and it sees a breakout coming. On 20 October the 10-year was at about 5.12% and the 30-year at about 5.43%, after weak housing starts. September CPI came in at 3.7% year on year, above forecasts.

The Plumbing

1. Reading a probability from fed funds futures

A 30-day fed funds future settles on the average effective fed funds rate over its contract month. A November contract lies wholly after the 28 October meeting, so its implied rate (100 minus the futures price) is the market’s expected policy rate after the decision. With one possible 25bp move:

P(hike) = (rimplied − rcurrent) ÷ 0.25%

illustrative: (3.925% − 3.880%) ÷ 0.25% = 18% hike ⇒ 82% hold

This is a risk-neutral probability. It includes any term premium in the contract, so it is not a pure forecast.

2. Why the 2-year is pinned and the 10-year isn’t

The 2-year yield is essentially the expected path of the policy rate over two years. With the next move priced as “hold now, maybe hike later”, it has little reason to move. The 10-year breaks down as follows:

y10 ≈ average expected short rate over 10 years + term premium

The term premium is the extra yield investors demand for locking money up for longer. It rises with inflation uncertainty, heavy Treasury issuance, and falling demand from price-insensitive buyers. With CPI at 3.7% and an IMF warning about a disorderly rise in yields, the long end is pricing uncertainty as well as the expected path. The 2s10s spread is about 33bp (5.115 − 4.785). A rise in yields led by the long end, where the long end rises more than the short end, is a bear steepener.

Why It Matters for a Fund Analyst

  • Look at key-rate duration, not just total duration. A “low-duration” bond fund can still hold a lot of 20–30 year exposure through a barbell.
  • Ask macro managers whether they are positioned for the curve (steepeners) or for the level of rates. Over the next week those are two different bets.
  • For multi-asset funds, check how much equity duration they hold: long-duration growth stocks are exposed to the term premium, not to the Fed.

Answers — tap here, or turn the page · Story II

4. B — 18%.

(3.925 − 3.880) ÷ 0.25 = 0.045 ÷ 0.25 = 0.18.

A reads the 4.5bp difference as a probability without dividing by the size of the move. C divides by 12.5bp. D is the probability of a hold.

5. C — about +$500k.

A steepener is long the 2-year and short the 10-year. Long 2-year: −5 × $50k = −$250k. Short 10-year: +15 × $50k = +$750k. Net +$500k: the curve steepened by 10bp.

A gets the sign wrong. B: being duration-neutral protects against parallel moves, not changes in slope. D adds the two moves instead of netting them.

6. B — more inflation uncertainty and more duration supply.

Both raise the compensation investors demand for holding duration, without changing where the policy rate is expected to go.

A and D change the expected path, which is the other part of the 10-year yield. C is a tool for steering the policy rate within its range, so it works on the front end, not the term premium.

Structured Credit · ETFs · Story III

The Daily-Liquid Wrapper on a Quarterly-Priced Asset

Investors piled into CLO ETFs in September. They bought floating-rate AAA paper, and with it a liquidity promise the underlying market does not make.

From our structured credit desk

The News

U20 Oct. — US-listed ETFs holding collateralised loan obligations took a record $3.3bn of inflows in September, bringing the year-to-date total to about $18bn, according to TD Securities data reported by The Daily Upside. CLO ETF assets have passed $50bn, and several products now hold AAA tranches of private credit CLOs.

The Plumbing

1. Rate duration versus spread duration

CLO tranches pay SOFR plus a spread, and the coupon resets quarterly. Their rate duration is therefore close to zero, which is why money has poured in while the 10-year sits above 5%. Their spread duration is not close to zero. It is roughly 3–5 years, depending on the reinvestment period and when the deal can be called.

ΔP / P ≈ −SD × Δs

illustrative: −4 × 0.40% = −1.6% for a 40bp spread widening

2. What protects a AAA tranche

The first line of protection is subordination, meaning all the tranches below the AAA that absorb losses first. Behind that are the overcollateralisation (OC) and interest coverage (IC) tests. If a test fails, cash is diverted away from equity and used to pay down the senior notes. The table shows how much of the loan pool would have to default before the AAA lost anything:

*Ignores excess spread and OC diversion, which would add further protection.

3. The wrapper

The ETF trades every second, but CLO tranches trade over the counter, and the fund’s NAV uses evaluated (model) prices for them. In a sell-off the ETF price moves first, and a discount to NAV opens. That discount is usually price discovery: the ETF is showing where tranches would actually trade before the evaluated prices catch up. Arbitrage cannot close it quickly. To profit, authorised participants would have to redeem shares and then sell the tranches into a market with wide bid-ask spreads.

Why It Matters for a Fund Analyst

  • Report spread duration next to rate duration. “Near-zero duration” is only half the risk.
  • Ask whether redemptions are met in kind or in cash, and how big the fund is relative to new AAA issuance.
  • Check exposure to private-credit CLOs, which have different collateral (middle-market loans with Level 3 marks) and less trading history.
  • Look at the premium or discount during past stress (e.g. March 2020, April 2025), not its average.

7. A AAA CLO ETF has a rate duration of 0.2 and a spread duration of 4. AAA CLO spreads widen 40bp while Treasury yields are unchanged. What is the approximate price change?

  1. About zero, because the tranches are floating rate.
  2. −0.08%
  3. −1.6%
  4. −16%

8. In a simplified CLO, the AAA tranche is 62% of the capital structure and recoveries are assumed at 60%. Ignoring excess spread and the coverage tests, roughly what cumulative default rate on the loan pool would start to impair the AAA?

  1. 38%
  2. 63%
  3. 95%
  4. 62%

9. In a credit sell-off, a AAA CLO ETF trades 2% below its NAV for several days. What is the most likely explanation?

  1. Several AAA tranches in the portfolio have defaulted.
  2. The NAV uses evaluated prices that lag the market, and arbitrage is costly when tranches are illiquid.
  3. Authorised participants are making riskless profits, so the gap must close within the day.
  4. The ETF has suspended redemptions.
Answers — tap here, or turn the page · Story III

7. C — about −1.6%.

Spread duration 4 × 0.40% = 1.6%. Treasury yields did not move, so rate duration plays no part here.

A confuses floating rate with risk-free. B applies the rate duration (0.2 × 0.40%) to a spread move. D slips a decimal point.

8. C — about 95%.

The AAA is impaired once pool losses exceed the 38% of subordination below it. Loss = default × (1 − recovery) = default × 40%, so default = 38% ÷ 0.40 = 95%.

A confuses defaults with losses. B divides by the recovery rate (60%) instead of loss given default (40%). D uses the AAA share instead of the subordination below it.

9. B — stale evaluated prices, and costly arbitrage.

The ETF price is the only real-time price for these tranches. NAV catches up as dealers re-mark them. Authorised participants won’t close the gap while selling tranches would cost more than the discount.

A: AAA CLO tranches have essentially never defaulted, and a 2% move is a spread move, not a credit loss. C: the profit is not riskless once bid-ask spreads and execution are paid. D: ETFs do not suspend redemptions the way a fund gates; the discount appears while redemptions are still open.
